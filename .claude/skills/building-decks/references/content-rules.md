# Content rules

A deck fails in the room on content long before it fails on CSS. These are the
editorial rules both reference decks are built on, each learned by getting it
wrong first.

## Numbers

**One claim, one number, defined once.** A deck once carried 100%, 87% and 75%
for the same completion claim on three different slides. Any of them could be
defended alone; together they destroy the argument.

- Pick the number, put it on exactly one slide, and put the denominator in the
  speaker note.
- If two slides both want a number, give them different *kinds* of number: one
  completion claim, one engagement claim. Never two versions of the same claim.

**A count beats a percentage when the count is small.** "66 of 66" reads as
honest and shows its own denominator. "100%" reads as marketing and invites the
question you do not want asked.

**Never print a number that names a shortfall nobody asked about.** "84%
attendance" also says 16% did not show up. Say it out loud if asked; do not put
it on a slide.

**Check what a number actually measures before printing it.** A "100% completion"
figure turned out to be every certificate in the system issued in three bulk
batches spanning 75 seconds. True, but not what a reader assumes.

**Cut a weak metric out of a screenshot rather than shrinking it.** A dashboard
tile reading "1 work submitted" next to "117 sessions attended" is the one a
reader's eye finds. Crop, do not edit — never alter a number.

## Citations

**Cite per claim, not per slide.** One citation line under two numbers from
different sources is a false citation for one of them. Attribute separately:

> Self-paced: MIT & Harvard on edX, 2012–2018 (Reich & Ruipérez-Valiente,
> Science 2019). Cohort-based: industry benchmark.

**Never attach a real citation to a number it does not contain.** If a figure
comes from an industry write-up rather than a paper, say "industry benchmark".
A false citation next to a real one is worse than no citation.

**Do not label someone else's benchmark as your result.** A row reading "Inside
our cohorts — 75%" where the 75% was an industry figure is the single most
dangerous kind of line in a deck.

## People

**Verify every face.** A supplied mock had five of seven people wrong, including
two sitting legislators. Ask, do not infer from layout position.

**Named people carry obligations.** Showing a sitting official by name and face
reads as endorsement. Get written permission and confirm the wording each is
comfortable with. Put that warning in the speaker note so whoever presents sees
it.

**Do not use a stock photo under a named person's story.** A card that names
someone, gives their job, and tells their transition is a claim. A stock face
makes it a false one. Either use their real photo or make the card a role rather
than a person.

**Watch for third parties in screenshots.** A product screenshot may include
learner names, absence records, or certificates with names on them. Crop above
them.

## Copy

**Say what the buyer's job is, not what your category is.** "Cohort program
platform" names what they do. "LXP" or "LMS" drops you into a feature checklist
against incumbents and is jargon outside the industry anyway.

**If sub-copy is too small to read from the back, delete it.** Three titles at
48px beat three titles with unreadable paragraphs beneath them. The presenter
says the rest.

**Long bios do not fit.** Either cut for the slide and put the full text in the
speaker note, or go three columns on a narrower portrait. Do not shrink past the
floor.

**Cut repetition between adjacent slides.** A closing slide repeating the
previous slide's call to action reads as busy, not emphatic.

## House style

Check the brand for these. BCC's rules, as an example:

- No em dashes. Use commas, colons, or full stops.
- Blue, yellow and white only. No black as a large surface.
- Lead with ambition, not risk.
- No self-set benchmarks or dates the organisation has not committed to.

## Flag rather than fix

Some problems are not yours to solve. Say them plainly once, then proceed with
what was asked:

- a number that contradicts another number
- a name or face you cannot verify
- a claim that needs someone's permission
- a stat with no source

Put the flag in the speaker note as well as saying it, so it survives into the
room.
